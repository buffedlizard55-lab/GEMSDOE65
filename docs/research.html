Research notes and the verification trail

What the published sources do and do not establish about Great Basin fault completeness, observability and PU identifiability.

What the competition actually asks

The official problem description says the target is fault presence as a structure indicative of geothermal resources. It explicitly notes that currently known public faults are incomplete and may contain inaccuracies, and that experts identified additional faults for evaluation. Therefore a hide-and-recover holdout against known catalogue faults is a necessary model-selection proxy, not direct evidence that the model finds truly unmapped faults.

The official submission contract requires a single-band float32 GeoTIFF, pixel confidence in [0, 1], the training grid's CRS/resolution/bounds, and null/NaN outside the study footprint. The metric is distance-weighted Tversky with alpha = 0.2, beta = 0.8, and a 300 m triangular support kernel. These are organizer-published task specifications—not a score for this project.

Why model catalogue inclusion

Giddens & Faulds' 2025 Great Basin step-over study reports that approximately 91.9% of identified step-overs are associated with range-front faults, while warning that steep topographic gradients make range-front step-overs easier to identify. The paper also says only about 25% of its study area was mapped in sufficient detail (for example, at 1:24,000 scale) and that lack of high-resolution fault, lidar, and detailed mapping likely caused smaller step-overs to be missed. These are published observations about their inventory and study area; they do not prove the same propensity function for every fault in the competition footprint.

That combination motivates a selection model: e(x)=P(S=1 | Y=1, X=x), where Y denotes an actual fault and S denotes inclusion in the labelled catalogue. Terrain relief/slope and DEM-source coverage describe observability; gravity-derived basin fill can describe burial/cover; range-front distance can describe a visibility regime. The paper's methods describe USGS one-meter lidar primarily over Walker Lane and an east-trending arm into north-central Nevada, with ten-meter 3DEP DEMs elsewhere in its study area. That motivates a source-coverage covariate, but does not verify which source or resolution covers each competition pixel; local coverage must be read from authoritative metadata. These covariates should model the chance an existing fault was mapped, not be mistaken for direct proof of a fault.

What PU theory does and does not identify

Bekker & Davis (PMLR, 2018) formalize the Selected At Random assumption and propose SAR-EM when propensity depends on a known subset of attributes. Their paper emphasizes that learning requires assumptions about class distributions and/or the labeling process. Bekker, Robberechts & Davis (arXiv:1809.03207; ECML-PKDD 2019) introduce an empirical-risk method incorporating a labeling mechanism and discuss learning when that mechanism is incompletely known. This supports the proposed comparison, but does not make the Great Basin propensity identifiable from a positive–unlabeled catalogue without external anchoring or sensitivity assumptions.

The requested weight cap of 10 is a project protocol choice, not a result from these papers. It should be applied identically and logged. If a quadrangle anchor is not defensible, the output of SAR-EM must be reported as a sensitivity analysis, not a calibrated map of true fault probability.

Map-coverage anchor: what is available

A public USGS 7.5-minute map-index service exists and includes 1:24,000 map-frame polygons. It is a reference grid of quadrangle extents, not a record that each quadrangle has exhaustive detailed geologic mapping. Separately, the USGS National Geologic Map Database / MapView catalogue lets users search geologic maps and provides publication metadata. The USGS describes the NGMDB as a large archive, while also stating that detailed geologic mapping has not been completed everywhere. A defensible anchor would therefore require a footprint-and-scale audit of individual map records, not simply labeling every indexed quadrangle as exhaustively mapped.

Decision: the public index is available as a candidate source, but no exhaustive-mapping indicator has been verified in this checkout. Use it only after testing catalog completeness, scale, map extent, and intersection with the exact competition footprint; otherwise keep propensity scale unanchored and report sensitivity.

External data lead: gravity / basin fill

The USGS published a free, CC0 data release for a three-dimensional hydrogeologic framework of the Great Basin that includes a gravity-based regional depth-to-basement surface. The official ScienceBase metadata lists a downloadable Surfaces.zip raster bundle, so the source is obtainable; it has not been downloaded in this checkout. The release describes the study as predominantly eastern Nevada and western Utah, gives a broad geographic bounding box, and describes model nodes spaced at one mile. Bounding boxes alone do not establish raster overlap with the northwestern GeoDAWN submission footprint, and the grid is much coarser than the competition grid. It is therefore conditionally testable only after an exact footprint intersection and resolution/quality audit; it is not a viable predictor yet. The safer first test is a predeclared proxy from the competition-provided gravity bands—once those files and the shared evaluator are present.

Interpretation boundary

A format-valid raster can still be geologically wrong. A holdout against visible catalogue faults can still fail to generalize to genuinely new faults. A public leaderboard score cannot be attributed to a named file without a receipt. These evidence types must remain separate.
